🧠“At a Glance” — the whole chapter in 20 points

  1. Ancient roots: Acharya Kanada called the smallest indivisible particles parmanu (Vaisesika Sutras); Leucippus and Democritus called them atomos (Greek for indivisible).
  2. Dalton (1808) gave the first scientific atomic theory: matter is made of indivisible atoms, the building blocks of matter.
  3. Radioactivity, discovered in the late 19th century, showed that atoms are made of smaller subatomic particles — Dalton's indivisible atom was over.
  4. Thomson (1897) discovered the electron in cathode rays; charge −1 (−1.602 × 10−19 C), mass about 1/1840 of a hydrogen atom.
  5. Thomson's model: a sphere of uniform positive charge with electrons embedded in it — the plum pudding (or watermelon) model.
  6. Gold foil experiment (1911) by Geiger and Marsden under Rutherford: most α-particles passed straight through, a few were sharply deflected, very few bounced back.
  7. Conclusions: the atom is mostly empty space; all the positive charge and most of the mass sit in a tiny, dense central nucleus. Atom ≈ 10−10 m, nucleus ≈ 10−15 m.
  8. Rutherford's limitations: he could not explain why the revolving electron does not lose energy and fall into the nucleus, and he did not describe the arrangement of electrons.
  9. Proton: discovered by Rutherford; charge +1, equal and opposite to the electron; in a neutral atom the number of protons equals the number of electrons.
  10. Bohr (1913): electrons revolve in fixed orbits (stationary states, shells K, L, M, N) and do not lose energy there; they jump between shells by absorbing or releasing a fixed amount of energy. Energy increases from K outwards.
  11. Neutron (1932): discovered by James Chadwick; mass nearly equal to a proton, charge zero; present in the nuclei of all atoms except ordinary hydrogen; it reduces proton–proton repulsion.
  12. Symbols: Dalton used pictorial symbols in 1803; Berzelius suggested letter symbols in 1813; IUPAC approves them today. First letter capital, second letter lowercase (Co, not CO).
  13. Atomic number Z = number of protons (= number of electrons in a neutral atom). Mass number A = protons + neutrons (nucleons). Neutrons = A − Z.
  14. Notation: mass number as superscript and atomic number as subscript on the left of the symbol — e.g. ¹²₆C.
  15. Bohr–Bury rules (1921): a shell holds a maximum of 2n² electrons (K = 2, L = 8, M = 18); the outermost shell holds a maximum of 8; shells fill stepwise from K outwards.
  16. Valence electrons live in the outermost shell and decide chemical properties. Valency is the combining capacity: ≤ 4 valence electrons → valency = that number; > 4 → valency = 8 − that number.
  17. Isotopes: same atomic number, different mass numbers (protium, deuterium, tritium; ¹²₆C, ¹³₆C, ¹⁴₆C). Same chemical properties, different physical properties.
  18. Isobars: different atomic numbers, same mass number (Ar, K and Ca all have A = 40).
  19. Average atomic mass is the weighted average over natural abundances: chlorine = (75/100 × 35) + (25/100 × 37) = 35.5 u, not the simple mean 36 u.
  20. Uses of isotopes: ²³⁵₉₂U in reactors, ⁶⁰₂₇Co in cancer therapy, ¹³¹₅₃I for goitre and thyroid cancer, ¹⁴₆C for dating fossils and artefacts.

📐Formula and fact sheet

📐Mass number
A = number of protons + number of neutrons
so neutrons = A − Z
📐Atomic number
Z = number of protons = number of electrons (neutral atom)
and protons = Z
📐Shell capacity
Maximum electrons in a shell = 2n²
K = 2, L = 8, M = 18, N = 32; outermost shell ≤ 8
📐Valency (from valence electrons)
valency = number of valence electrons (if ≤ 4); valency = 8 − valence electrons (if > 4)
noble gases: valency 0
📐Average atomic mass
(Σ abundance% × isotope mass) ÷ 100
chlorine: (75×35 + 25×37)/100 = 35.5 u
📐Size of the atom
atom ≈ 10−10 m, nucleus ≈ 10−15 m
nucleus is about 105 times smaller
📐Charge of an electron
−1.602 × 10−19 C, taken as −1 unit
proton: +1 · neutron: 0

🔭Threads of Curiosity — questions that stretch your thinking

Q1Why are the shells called K, L, M, N and not A, B, C, D?▾

The letters come from the X-ray naming system of the scientist Charles Barkla. When he studied X-rays, he labelled the strongest line K and the next ones L, M, N… Bohr borrowed this convenient notation, so the first shell of an atom became the K-shell.

Q2If protons repel each other, why doesn't the nucleus break apart?▾

Because of the nuclear force — a strong attractive force between nucleons that is much stronger than the electrostatic repulsion at very short distances. Neutrons are very useful here: they increase the distance between the repelling protons and add to the nuclear force, which makes heavier nuclei more stable.

Q3Why does nature mix isotopes instead of giving each element one mass?▾

Isotopes form in stars and in nuclear reactions with different probabilities, so most elements occur in nature as a mixture. That mixture is why atomic masses in the periodic table are fractional — like chlorine's 35.5 u.

🧪Ready to Go Beyond?

Q1What if the gold foil were much thicker?▾
A thicker foil would mean more layers of nuclei in the path of each alpha particle, so many more particles would be deflected. Extremely thin foil was chosen so that most alpha particles would meet as few atoms as possible.
Q2What came after Bohr?▾
Bohr's model could explain hydrogen's spectrum but not the finer details of heavier atoms. Around 1925 the quantum mechanical model replaced it, describing electrons as an electron cloud — a region of probability rather than a fixed path. This is the model used by chemists today.
Q3The atomic age.▾
Chadwick's neutron (1932) soon became a tool for nuclear reactions: neutrons, being neutral, can enter a nucleus easily. This led to nuclear fission, reactors and also, unfortunately, to nuclear weapons — a reminder that science must be used responsibly.
Q4Seeing atoms.▾
The Scanning Tunnelling Microscope (STM) and Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) can image individual atoms, making the atom something we can picture and even move, atom by atom.

👨‍🔬Meet the Scientists

🔬J. J. Thomson

Discovered the electron · Nobel Prize 1906

Showed in 1897 that cathode rays are streams of electrons, that electrons are part of every atom, and proposed the plum pudding model.

🥇Ernest Rutherford

Gold foil experiment · Nobel Prize 1908

Discovered the nucleus and the proton, and proposed the nuclear (planetary) model of the atom in 1911.

🛰️Niels Bohr

Shell model of the atom · Nobel Prize 1922

In 1913 he introduced fixed energy shells (stationary states) and explained why atoms are stable.

⚪James Chadwick

Discovered the neutron · Nobel Prize 1935

In 1932 he found the neutral particle in the nucleus whose mass is nearly equal to a proton's.

🏺Acharya Kanada

The idea of the parmanu

An ancient Indian thinker who reasoned that matter can be divided only up to a limit — the indivisible parmanu — recorded in the Vaisesika Sutras.

📡Charles Barkla

Gave the K, L, M notation

His X-ray naming of spectral lines gave Bohr the letters K, L, M, N for the shells — which is why the first shell is K.

🇮🇳India's Scientific Contributions

🇮🇳 Ancient India: Acharya Kanada's parmanu

Centuries before modern chemistry, Indian thinkers in the Vaisesika school proposed that matter is made of indivisible particles and that these combine in pairs and triplets to build all substances.

⚛️ Homi J. Bhabha and India's atomic programme

Homi Bhabha founded the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC) at Trombay, the cradle of India's peaceful nuclear research, including reactors such as Dhruva that use isotopes and nuclear reactions for medicine, agriculture and power.

🏥 Isotopes in Indian medicine

Radioisotopes such as cobalt-60 and iodine-131 are used widely in Indian hospitals for cancer therapy and diagnosis, and radiation technology is used to preserve food and improve crop varieties.

📖Glossary — every important word of the chapter

TermMeaning
AtomThe smallest particle of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction; made of electrons, protons and neutrons.
ParmanuThe name Acharya Kanada gave to the smallest indivisible particle of matter.
AtomosGreek word meaning “indivisible”, used by Leucippus and Democritus.
RadioactivityThe spontaneous emission of invisible energy and particles from the nucleus of certain elements.
Cathode raysStreams of electrons travelling from the cathode to the anode in a discharge tube.
Electron (e⁻)Negatively charged subatomic particle, charge −1, mass 1/1840 u, moving in shells around the nucleus.
Proton (p⁺)Positively charged subatomic particle of charge +1 and mass 1 u, present in the nucleus.
Neutron (n⁰)Neutral subatomic particle of mass about 1 u, present in the nucleus of all atoms except ordinary hydrogen.
NucleusThe extremely small, dense, positively charged central part of an atom that contains almost all its mass.
Alpha particleA positively charged particle identical to a helium nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons), charge +2.
ScatteringThe deflection of particles when they strike or pass close to another particle. The gold foil experiment is an α-ray scattering experiment.
Plum pudding modelThomson's model: a sphere of uniform positive charge with electrons embedded throughout it.
Planetary modelRutherford's model: electrons orbiting a central nucleus like planets around the Sun.
Stationary stateA fixed orbit (shell) in Bohr's model in which an electron neither gains nor loses energy.
Shell / orbit / energy levelA fixed path around the nucleus, named K, L, M, N (n = 1, 2, 3, 4).
NucleonA proton or a neutron — a particle that lives in the nucleus.
Atomic number (Z)The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom; it is the identity of the element.
Mass number (A)The total number of protons and neutrons (nucleons) in the nucleus.
Electronic configurationThe distribution of electrons in the shells of an atom, e.g. 2, 8, 1 for sodium.
Valence shellThe outermost shell of an atom.
Valence electronsThe electrons present in the outermost shell; they take part in bonding.
ValencyThe combining capacity of an atom — the number of electrons it loses, gains or shares to complete its octet.
OctetA complete set of 8 electrons in the outermost shell, which makes an atom stable.
IsotopesAtoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers.
IsobarsAtoms of different elements with the same mass number but different atomic numbers.
RadioisotopeAn unstable isotope that decays by emitting α or β particles and γ rays, e.g. carbon-14.
Average atomic massThe weighted average of the masses of all naturally occurring isotopes of an element.
Unified atomic mass unit (u)One-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom; used to express atomic masses.
Electron cloudThe modern (quantum mechanical) picture of an atom in which electrons are described by the probability of being found around the nucleus.
