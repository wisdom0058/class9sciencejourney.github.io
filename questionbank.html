🔗Section 2 — Assertion–Reason questions

AR1Assertion (A): Atoms are electrically neutral. Reason (R): In an atom the number of protons equals the number of electrons.

Option (i): Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Atoms have no net charge because the positive charge of the protons exactly balances the negative charge of the electrons. That balance is the reason for neutrality, so R correctly explains A.

Assertion–Reason
AR2Assertion (A): Chlorine has an atomic mass of 35.5 u, although its mass number is taken as 35. Reason (R): Chlorine occurs in nature as a mixture of isotopes, so its atomic mass is a weighted average.

Option (i): Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Natural chlorine is 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37. The weighted average (75/100 × 35) + (25/100 × 37) = 35.5 u. Mass number is a whole-number count of nucleons in one particular atom, so it is 35 (or 37), never 35.5.

Assertion–Reason
AR3Assertion (A): The outermost shell of an atom can hold a maximum of 8 electrons. Reason (R): The formula 2n² gives the maximum electrons in any shell.

Option (ii): Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.

2n² gives the capacity of a shell (K = 2, L = 8, M = 18, N = 32), but the Bohr–Bury rule separately limits the outermost shell to 8 electrons even when 2n² allows more. So R states a true fact but does not explain the 8-electron limit.

Assertion–Reason
AR4Assertion (A): Sodium has valency 1. Reason (R): Sodium has one electron in its outermost shell, which it loses to complete its octet.

Option (i): Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Sodium's configuration is 2, 8, 1. With only one valence electron it loses that electron to reach the stable configuration 2, 8. The number of electrons lost is its valency, so valency = 1.

Assertion–Reason
AR5Assertion (A): The gold foil experiment disproved Thomson's model. Reason (R): In the gold foil experiment most alpha particles passed straight through the foil.

Option (ii): Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.

Most alpha particles passing straight through shows that the atom is mostly empty space — which the plum pudding model could also allow. What actually disproved Thomson's model was the bouncing back of a few alpha particles, which is impossible if the positive charge is spread out evenly.

⚡Section 3 — Very short answer questions (20)

V1Who first used the term 'atomos' and what does it mean?

Leucippus and Democritus used the term atomos, which means indivisible in Greek.

V2What did Acharya Kanada call the smallest indivisible particles of matter?

He called them parmanus. His ideas are recorded in the Sanskrit text Vaisesika Sutras.

V3In which year did John Dalton propose his atomic theory, and what was its main idea?

In 1808. He proposed that all matter is composed of indivisible particles called atoms, the fundamental building blocks of matter.

V4What are cathode rays?

Streams of negatively charged particles (electrons) that move from the cathode (negative electrode) to the anode (positive electrode) in a discharge tube.

V5What is the charge of an electron?

−1.602 × 10−19 C, which is taken as −1 as a matter of convention.

V6Why is Thomson's model called the plum pudding model?

Because he proposed the atom as a sphere of positive charge with electrons distributed throughout it, just like plums embedded in a pudding.

V7What is the gold foil experiment also known as, and who performed it?

It is also called the α-ray scattering experiment. It was performed by Geiger and Marsden under Ernest Rutherford in 1911.

V8What is the nucleus of an atom?

The extremely small, dense, positively charged region at the centre of an atom where most of the atom's mass is concentrated.

V9State the main limitation of Rutherford's model of the atom.

It could not explain the stability of the atom: an accelerating electron should lose energy and spiral into the nucleus, but atoms do not collapse.

V10Who discovered the proton, and what is its charge relative to the electron?

Rutherford discovered and named the proton. Its charge is equal and opposite to that of an electron, taken as +1.

V11What are the stationary orbits (shells) in Bohr's model of the atom?

Fixed circular paths called stationary states, orbits or shells (K, L, M, N) in which an electron does not lose energy.

V12Who discovered the neutron, and in which year?

James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932. It is a neutral particle with mass nearly equal to that of a proton.

V13What is the atomic number of an element? Give its symbol.

The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom of the element. It is denoted by Z.

V14Write the formula for mass number and define nucleons.

Mass number (A) = number of protons + number of neutrons. Protons and neutrons together are called nucleons.

V15What is the maximum number of electrons that can be filled in the K, L and M shells?

Using 2n²: K (n = 1) = 2, L (n = 2) = 8, M (n = 3) = 18 electrons.

V16What are valence electrons?

The electrons present in the outermost shell of an atom are called valence electrons; that shell is called the valence shell.

V17Define the valency of an element.

Valency is the combining capacity of an atom — the number of electrons it gains, loses or shares to complete its octet and become stable.

V18What are isotopes? Give one example.

Atoms of the same element having the same atomic number but different mass numbers. Example: hydrogen has protium (¹₁H), deuterium (²₁H) and tritium (³₁H).

V19What are isobars? Give an example.

Atoms of different elements having the same mass number but different atomic numbers. Example: calcium (Z = 20), potassium (Z = 19) and argon (Z = 18) all have mass number 40.

V20Give one use each of the isotopes carbon-14 and iodine-131.

¹⁴₆C is used in archaeology and geology to find the age of ancient fossils and artefacts; ¹³¹₅₃I is used to treat goitre and thyroid cancer.

📝Section 4 — Short answer questions

S1What were the contributions of Acharya Kanada and of the Greek philosophers Leucippus and Democritus to the concept of the atom?

Acharya Kanada, an ancient Indian thinker, proposed that if matter (dravya) is divided repeatedly, a stage is reached where the smallest indivisible particles remain. He called these parmanus and recorded his ideas in the Vaisesika Sutras. According to him a parmanu is infinitely small and cannot be perceived by the senses, and combinations of parmanus in pairs (dyads) and triplets (triads) form all material substances.

The Greek philosophers Leucippus and Democritus proposed that matter consists of tiny indivisible particles which they named atomos — a Greek word meaning indivisible. Both traditions arrived at remarkably parallel ideas, although neither was based on experimental observations.

S2How did J. J. Thomson discover the electron and what conclusion did he draw about the nature of cathode rays?

In 1897 Thomson studied the conduction of electric current through gases at very low pressure using a glass tube with two electrodes and a high voltage. He observed rays travelling from the cathode to the anode — cathode rays.

Studying them in electric and magnetic fields, he concluded that they are streams of negatively charged particles with a mass much smaller than that of atoms, and named them electrons. Since the rays were independent of the cathode material and the gas used, electrons are a fundamental component of all atoms. The charge of an electron is taken as −1 (−1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C).

S3Describe Thomson's model of the atom. Why is it called the plum pudding model?

After discovering electrons, Thomson faced a puzzle: electrons are negatively charged while atoms are electrically neutral, so positive charge must exist somewhere. He proposed that an atom is a sphere of uniform positive charge with electrons distributed throughout it.

The model was compared to a pudding with plums embedded in it — the positive sphere acting like the pudding and the electrons like the plums. Hence the name plum pudding model. A simpler comparison is a watermelon, where the red pulp represents the positive charge and the seeds represent the electrons. It was the first genuine attempt to explain how positive and negative charges stay balanced, but Rutherford's gold foil experiment later disproved it.

S4What is radioactivity? How did its discovery challenge Dalton's atomic theory?

In the late 19th century scientists discovered that certain elements emit invisible energy and particles spontaneously. This phenomenon is called radioactivity.

Until then atoms were believed to be the smallest indivisible units of matter, as Dalton had proposed. But radioactivity showed that particles and energy are emitted from within the atom itself, so atoms must be made of even smaller subatomic particles. This directly challenged Dalton's theory and laid the foundation for further investigation by scientists such as Thomson and Rutherford.

S5How did Rutherford discover the proton? How does the presence of protons explain the electrical neutrality of atoms?

Rutherford showed through the gold foil experiment that the nucleus of an atom carries positive charge. He identified the particles responsible for this positive charge and named them protons. Protons are much heavier than electrons and carry a charge equal in magnitude but opposite in sign to that of an electron; the relative charge of a proton is +1.

For an atom to be electrically neutral, the total positive charge from protons must exactly balance the total negative charge from electrons — that is, number of protons = number of electrons. For example a helium atom has 2 protons and 2 electrons, and a sodium atom has 11 protons and 11 electrons. In both cases the charges cancel out completely.

S6According to Bohr's model, how does an electron move from one shell to another? How does the energy of shells change as we move away from the nucleus?

Electrons revolve in fixed circular paths called stationary states or shells (K, L, M, N for n = 1, 2, 3, 4). An electron in a stationary state does not lose energy while revolving.

Movement between shells: an electron can move from one shell to another by absorbing or releasing a fixed amount of energy equal to the difference in energy between the two shells. If energy is absorbed it jumps to a higher shell (farther from the nucleus); if energy is released it falls back to a lower shell.

Energy with distance: the energy of shells increases as we move away from the nucleus. The K-shell (n = 1), being closest, has the least energy; L has more than K, and so on.

S7How was the neutron discovered? What role does it play in the nucleus?

The neutron was discovered in 1932 by James Chadwick, a student of Rutherford. Scientists had noticed a puzzle: a helium atom has two protons yet its mass is about four times that of a hydrogen atom, not double. This suggested another particle in the nucleus that adds mass without adding charge.

Chadwick discovered a new subatomic particle with a mass nearly equal to a proton but with no electrical charge — the neutron, symbol n⁰. Neutrons are found in the nucleus of all atoms except hydrogen. Along with protons they give the atom its mass, and they help reduce the repulsion between positive protons by increasing the spacing between them and strengthening the nuclear force that holds the nucleus together.

S8What are atomic number and mass number? Write the standard notation used to represent an atom, with an example.

The atomic number (Z) is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. Since atoms are neutral, the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so Z also gives the number of electrons.

The mass number (A) is the total number of protons and neutrons (nucleons) in the nucleus: A = number of protons + number of neutrons.

Notation: the mass number is written at the top-left and the atomic number at the bottom-left of the element's symbol: AZX. For example carbon with Z = 6 and A = 12 is written as ¹²₆C.

S9What are the rules followed by Bohr and Bury for distributing electrons in the shells of an atom?

(1) The maximum number of electrons in any shell is given by 2n²: K-shell (n = 1) holds up to 2, L-shell up to 8 and M-shell up to 18.

(2) The maximum number of electrons in the outermost shell is 8, except for the first shell, which can hold a maximum of 2.

(3) Electrons are filled in a stepwise manner, starting from the shell closest to the nucleus (K), then L, M, N, and so on. The next shell begins filling only after the inner shell is complete.

S10What is valency? Explain with the examples of sodium and oxygen how valency is determined from the electronic configuration.

The valency of an element is the number of electrons an atom gains, loses or shares to complete its outermost shell (octet) and attain a stable configuration — its combining capacity.

Sodium (2, 8, 1): the outermost shell has only 1 electron. Since fewer than 4 electrons are present in the valence shell, sodium tends to lose 1 electron to achieve an octet, so valency = 1.

Oxygen (2, 6): the outermost shell has 6 electrons. Since more than 4 valence electrons are present, oxygen tends to gain 2 electrons to complete its octet, so valency = 2.

S11What is average atomic mass? Chlorine exists as two isotopes ³⁵₁₇Cl (75%) and ³⁷₁₇Cl (25%). Calculate the weighted average atomic mass of chlorine.

The average atomic mass of a natural element is the weighted average of the masses of all its naturally occurring isotopes, calculated by considering their relative (percent) abundances. It is more accurate than a simple arithmetic mean because isotopes do not occur in equal proportions.

Calculation: (75/100 × 35) + (25/100 × 37) = 26.25 + 9.25 = 35.5 u. Note that no single chlorine atom has a mass of 35.5 u — this value is an average over a large number of atoms as they occur in nature.

S12What is Dalton's atomic theory? How did it serve as the starting point for understanding atomic structure?

In 1808 John Dalton proposed his atomic theory based on the scientific experiments of that time. Its key ideas: all matter is composed of extremely small particles called atoms; atoms are indivisible and cannot be broken into smaller parts; atoms are the fundamental building blocks of matter; atoms of the same element are identical while atoms of different elements are different.

It was the first scientific description of how matter is made, replacing the earlier purely imaginative ideas of parmanu and atomos with ideas grounded in experimental evidence. It became the starting point for our present understanding because it made scientists ask further questions — what are atoms made of, what do they look like, what makes atoms of one element different from another — whose pursuit led to the discovery of subatomic particles and modern atomic models.

📘Section 5 — Long answer questions

L1Describe Rutherford's model of the atom. What conclusions did he draw from the gold foil experiment? Mention any two limitations of this model.

From the results of the gold foil experiment Rutherford proposed the nuclear model (also called the planetary model) of the atom. His conclusions were:

  • Most of the atom is empty space, since most α-particles passed through the gold foil without deflection.
  • All the positive charge and most of the mass of the atom are concentrated in an extremely small, dense central region called the nucleus.
  • Electrons revolve around the nucleus in circular orbits, somewhat like planets revolving around the Sun — hence the name planetary model.
  • The nucleus is about 105 times smaller than the atom: the atom's diameter is about 10−10 m while the nucleus's is about 10−15 m.
  • Rutherford also discovered the proton: the nucleus carries positive charge due to protons, which are much heavier than electrons and have an equal but opposite charge. For neutrality the number of protons must equal the number of electrons.

Limitations:

  • Could not explain atomic stability. A revolving electron is always accelerating, and an accelerating charged particle must continuously lose energy by emitting radiation. The electron should therefore spiral inward and collapse into the nucleus — but atoms are stable.
  • Did not describe the arrangement of electrons around the nucleus, nor the specific energies that electrons possess.

L3Explain the rules for writing symbols of elements as per IUPAC norms. Why are standard symbols necessary? Give five examples of elements whose symbols are derived from languages other than English.

John Dalton introduced symbols for elements in 1803 using pictorial representations. In 1813 Berzelius suggested that symbols should be derived from the Latin names of elements, which gave rise to alphabetic chemical symbols. Today the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) approves all names and symbols.

Rules (IUPAC norms):

  • Many symbols are the first letter or the first two letters of the element's name (e.g. Hydrogen → H, Aluminium → Al).
  • The first letter is always a capital (uppercase) letter and the second letter, if any, is written in lowercase — Cobalt → Co (not CO), Aluminium → Al (not AL).
  • Some symbols are formed from the first letter and a letter other than the second letter of the name — Chlorine → Cl, Zinc → Zn.
  • Some symbols come from the Latin, Greek or German names of the elements instead of their English names.

Five examples from non-English names: Iron → Fe (Latin Ferrum), Copper → Cu (Latin Cuprum), Gold → Au (Latin Aurum), Mercury → Hg (Greek Hydrargyros), Tungsten → W (German Wolfram).

Necessity: standard symbols are internationally recognised and let scientists communicate clearly regardless of language barriers. If every scientist used different symbols for the same element there would be enormous confusion in research and industry.

Long answer
L4Define isotopes and isobars. Write the electronic configuration of the three isotopes of hydrogen. State two similarities and two differences between isotopes and isobars.

Isotopes: atoms of the same element having the same atomic number (Z) but different mass numbers (A) due to a different number of neutrons. Isobars: atoms of different elements that have the same mass number (A) but different atomic numbers (Z).

IsotopeSymbolProtonsNeutronsElectronsElectronic config.
Protium¹₁H101K: 1
Deuterium²₁H111K: 1
Tritium³₁H121K: 1

All three have the same electronic configuration (1 electron in the K-shell) and hence the same chemical properties.

Two similarities: both isotopes and isobars are atoms that differ in their nuclear composition (number of neutrons or protons); both are subclassifications of atoms used to explain why atoms of the same or different elements can have varying masses.

Two differences: isotopes have the same atomic number but different mass numbers, while isobars have different atomic numbers but the same mass number.

Long answer
L5An atom of an element X has a mass number of 31 and 16 neutrons in its nucleus. (i) Find the atomic number of X. (ii) Identify the element X. (iii) Write its electronic configuration. (iv) Find the number of valence electrons and determine its valency. (v) Write the standard notation for the atom of element X.

(i) A = number of protons + number of neutrons, so 31 = Z + 16, giving Z = 31 − 16 = 15.

(ii) The element with atomic number 15 is phosphorus (P).

(iii) Total electrons = 15: K-shell = 2, L-shell = 8, M-shell = 5, so the electronic configuration is 2, 8, 5.

(iv) The outermost (M) shell contains 5 valence electrons. Since this is more than 4, phosphorus tends to gain 3 electrons to complete its octet of 8. So the valency of phosphorus is 3.

(v) Standard notation: ³¹₁₅P.

Long answer

📄Section 6 — Case-based / picture-based questions

C1Rahul and Maya are playing a card game called “Chemical Connection”. Each card shows an atom with its atomic number and mass number. Maya picks a card showing ³⁵₁₇Cl and Rahul picks ³⁷₁₇Cl. (a) How many neutrons does each card show? (b) Are these two cards isotopes or isobars? (c) What is the approximate percentage ratio in which these two kinds of atoms occur in nature?

(a) Neutrons = A − Z. For ³⁵₁₇Cl: 35 − 17 = 18 neutrons. For ³⁷₁₇Cl: 37 − 17 = 20 neutrons.

(b) Both have Z = 17 (chlorine) but different mass numbers, so they are isotopes of chlorine.

(c) Naturally occurring chlorine is about 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37, a ratio of 3 : 1. That is why the average atomic mass is (75/100 × 35) + (25/100 × 37) = 35.5 u.

Case-based
C2Four students draw the shell diagrams of four atoms. Ananya draws 2, 8, 1; Bhavya draws 2, 8, 7; Chirag draws 2, 8, 8; Dhruv draws 2, 1. (a) Which students have atoms with the same number of shells? (b) Which atom will not take part in a chemical reaction, and why? (c) Write the valency of the element drawn by Bhavya.

(a) Ananya (2, 8, 1), Bhavya (2, 8, 7) and Chirag (2, 8, 8) all have three shells (K, L, M). Dhruv's atom (2, 1) has only two shells.

(b) Chirag's atom (2, 8, 8) will not react. Its outermost shell already has a complete octet of 8 electrons, so it has no need to lose, gain or share electrons — like the noble gas argon (Z = 18).

(c) Bhavya's atom has configuration 2, 8, 7, so it has 7 valence electrons. Since that is more than 4, valency = 8 − 7 = 1 (this is chlorine, which gains one electron).

Case-based
C3Two atoms P and Q are found to have mass numbers of 40 each, but P has 20 protons while Q has 18 protons. (a) Find the number of neutrons in each. (b) Identify P and Q. (c) What is the special name for such a pair of atoms?

(a) P: neutrons = 40 − 20 = 20. Q: neutrons = 40 − 18 = 22.

(b) Z = 20 is calcium (Ca) and Z = 18 is argon (Ar).

(c) Atoms of different elements having the same mass number but different atomic numbers are called isobars. (Potassium, Z = 19, also has mass number 40, so all three are isobars.)

Case-based

🔢Section 7 — Extra numericals

N1An element has 2 electrons in its outermost M-shell. (i) What is its atomic number? (ii) Write its electronic configuration. (iii) What is its valency?

(i) If M is the outermost shell with 2 electrons, the shells are filled as K = 2, L = 8, M = 2. Total electrons = 12, so the atomic number is 12 (magnesium, Mg).

(ii) Electronic configuration: 2, 8, 2.

(iii) The atom has 2 valence electrons, fewer than 4, so it loses 2 electrons → valency 2.

Numerical
N2Calculate the number of neutrons in ²³⁵₉₂U (uranium-235), given that it has 92 protons.

Neutrons = A − Z = 235 − 92 = 143 neutrons.

Numerical
N3Boron occurs in nature as two isotopes: ¹⁰₅B (20%) and ¹¹₅B (80%). Calculate its average atomic mass.

Average atomic mass = (10 × 20/100) + (11 × 80/100) = 2 + 8.8 = 10.8 u.

Numerical
N4Which of the two atoms ³⁵₁₇Cl and ³⁷₁₇Cl has more neutrons, and by how many?

³⁵₁₇Cl: neutrons = 35 − 17 = 18. ³⁷₁₇Cl: neutrons = 37 − 17 = 20.

So ³⁷₁₇Cl has 2 neutrons more than ³⁵₁₇Cl. They are isotopes of chlorine.

Numerical
N5The atomic number of an element is 16. (i) Write its electronic configuration. (ii) How many valence electrons does it have? (iii) What is its valency?

(i) Z = 16 → K = 2, L = 8, M = 6, so configuration = 2, 8, 6 (sulfur).

(ii) The outermost shell has 6 valence electrons.

(iii) Since the valence electrons are more than 4, valency = 8 − 6 = 2.

Numerical
N6An atom of an element has 8 neutrons and its mass number is 15. Find its atomic number, identify the element, write its electronic configuration and state its valency.

Atomic number = A − neutrons = 15 − 8 = 7, so the element is nitrogen (N).

Electronic configuration: 7 electrons → 2, 5.

Valency: 5 valence electrons (more than 4) → 8 − 5 = 3.

Numerical
