1 Choose the correct options and explain the reason for the correct and incorrect options in the context of Ernest Rutherford's gold foil experiment:
(i) The experiment clearly showed the existence of neutrons in the nucleus.
(ii) The results disproved the plum pudding model and led to the idea of a nucleus at the centre of the atom.
(iii) The large deflection of a few alpha particles indicated that most of the mass of the atom and positive charge are packed into a tiny centre.
(iv) The way alpha particles were deflected showed that electrons move around the nucleus.

(i) INCORRECT. The gold foil experiment did not show the existence of neutrons. Neutrons were discovered by James Chadwick in 1932, much later and through a completely different experiment. The gold foil experiment only revealed the existence and nature of the nucleus — that it is positively charged and dense.

(ii) CORRECT. The results — most alpha particles passing undeflected, some sharply deflected, a very few bouncing back — could not be explained by Thomson's plum pudding model, where the positive charge is spread out. They disproved that model and led Rutherford to propose a small, dense, positively charged nucleus at the centre of the atom.

(iii) CORRECT. A few alpha particles bounced back sharply. This is possible only if they met a very small but extremely dense and strongly positive region. If the positive charge were spread out, no alpha particle would bounce back. So the large deflection of a few alpha particles confirmed that most of the mass and all the positive charge are packed into a tiny centre — the nucleus.

(iv) INCORRECT. The experiment did not show or study the movement of electrons around the nucleus. It only gave information about the nucleus — its size, charge and density. Electrons moving in orbits was a logical proposal of Rutherford's model, not a direct demonstration of the experiment.

Revise, Reflect, Refine · Q1
2 Which of the following statements are correct or incorrect according to Bohr's atomic model? Give a reason for each statement.
(i) Electrons lose energy while moving in fixed orbits and slowly fall into the nucleus.
(ii) Electrons can exist anywhere around the nucleus with no fixed energy.
(iii) Electrons revolve around the nucleus in orbits of fixed energy without losing energy.
(iv) Electrons can be found between energy levels as they move around the nucleus.

(i) INCORRECT. In Bohr's model electrons do not lose energy while moving in fixed orbits. The key feature of the model is the idea of stationary states: when an electron is in a fixed orbit (shell), its energy stays constant and it does not radiate energy. So it does not spiral inward or fall into the nucleus.

(ii) INCORRECT. Electrons cannot exist anywhere around the nucleus freely. They must follow fixed circular paths called orbits or shells (K, L, M, N …), each with a definite, fixed amount of energy. An electron must be in one of these energy levels; it cannot float around with no fixed energy.

(iii) CORRECT. This statement correctly describes Bohr's model: electrons revolve around the nucleus in fixed circular orbits (stationary states) and do not lose energy while revolving. Their energy stays constant in a given orbit. This is exactly why Bohr's model could explain atomic stability, unlike Rutherford's model.

(iv) INCORRECT. Electrons cannot be found between energy levels. They can exist only in the fixed shells. An electron can move from one shell to another by absorbing or releasing a fixed amount of energy, but it cannot stay in the space between two levels — it jumps directly from one level to another.

Revise, Reflect, Refine · Q2
3 The composition of the nuclei of three atomic species X, Y and Z is given as follows:
  • X — 18 protons, 19 neutrons
  • Y — 17 protons, 18 neutrons
  • Z — 17 protons, 20 neutrons
Explain the relation between the following: (i) Y and Z   (ii) Z and X

First let us work out the mass numbers:

  • X: protons = 18, neutrons = 19 → mass number = 18 + 19 = 37
  • Y: protons = 17, neutrons = 18 → mass number = 17 + 18 = 35
  • Z: protons = 17, neutrons = 20 → mass number = 17 + 20 = 37

(i) Y and Z: both Y and Z have the same number of protons (atomic number = 17), which means both belong to the same element — chlorine (Cl). But their mass numbers are different (Y = 35, Z = 37). Therefore Y and Z are isotopes of each other: atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

(ii) Z and X: Z has atomic number 17 (chlorine) while X has atomic number 18 (argon), so they are different elements. However, both Z and X have the same mass number = 37. Atoms of different elements having the same mass number but different atomic numbers are called isobars. Therefore Z and X are isobars.

Revise, Reflect, Refine · Q3
4 What conclusion did Rutherford draw about the position and characteristics of the atom's positively charged part, based on the few alpha particles that bounced back or were deflected at large angles in the gold foil experiment?

Based on the few alpha particles that bounced back or were deflected at large angles, Rutherford drew these conclusions about the positively charged part of the atom:

  • The positive charge of the atom is not spread throughout the volume of the atom (as Thomson suggested) but is concentrated in an extremely small region at the centre. This small central positively charged region is called the nucleus.
  • The nucleus is extremely dense — it contains most of the mass of the atom packed into a very tiny volume.
  • The nucleus is very small compared with the atom: the diameter of the atom is about 10−10 m while the diameter of the nucleus is about 10−15 m, so the nucleus is about 105 (one lakh) times smaller.
  • Since most alpha particles passed straight through undeflected, most of an atom is empty space.
  • Only when an alpha particle came very close to the nucleus was it repelled strongly enough to bounce back or deflect at a large angle.
Revise, Reflect, Refine · Q4
5 Explain and arrange the following statements in the correct chronological order to show how atomic models have evolved over time:
(i) Bohr's model proposed that electrons move in fixed orbits around the nucleus, each with a definite energy.
(ii) Thomson's model depicted the atom as a 'plum pudding' with electrons embedded in a sphere of positive charge.
(iii) Rutherford's model proposed that atoms have a dense central nucleus.
(iv) Dalton's model described atoms as indivisible particles.

Correct chronological order: (iv) → (ii) → (iii) → (i)

  1. (iv) Dalton's model — 1808: Dalton described atoms as indivisible particles, the fundamental building blocks of matter that cannot be broken down further. This was the first scientific atomic theory, based on the experiments of that time.
  2. (ii) Thomson's model — 1897 onwards: after discovering the electron, Thomson proposed that the atom is a sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded throughout it (the plum pudding model). This was the first model to accept that atoms contain smaller charged particles.
  3. (iii) Rutherford's model — 1911: after the gold foil experiment, Rutherford proposed the planetary model — a small, dense, positively charged nucleus at the centre with electrons revolving around it like planets around the Sun. This disproved Thomson's model.
  4. (i) Bohr's model — 1913: to explain why electrons do not lose energy and fall into the nucleus (a limitation of Rutherford's model), Niels Bohr proposed that electrons move in fixed circular orbits (shells) of definite energy called stationary states.
Revise, Reflect, Refine · Q5
6 Electrons move around the nucleus in orbits. Why do they not fly away from the atom? Explain what keeps them attracted to the nucleus.

Electrons do not fly away from the atom because they are held in place by the electrostatic force of attraction between the negatively charged electrons and the positively charged protons in the nucleus. This attractive force acts like the force that keeps a planet in orbit around the Sun — it continuously pulls the electron towards the nucleus and prevents it from flying away in a straight line.

According to Bohr's model, electrons revolve in fixed circular orbits (stationary states). In these fixed orbits, the centripetal force needed to keep the electron moving in a circle is provided by the electrostatic attraction between the electron and the nucleus. As long as the electron remains in its allowed orbit, it does not lose energy and it neither flies away nor falls inward.

So the attractive electrostatic force between the oppositely charged electron and nucleus is what keeps the electron orbiting and stops it from flying away.

Revise, Reflect, Refine · Q6
7 Assertion (A): The discovery of subatomic particles helped in understanding the atomic structure.  Reason (R): The number of electrons is equal to the number of protons in an atom.
Choose the correct option:
(i) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
(ii) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
(iii) A is true, but R is false.
(iv) A is false, but R is true.

Option (ii): Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.

The Assertion is true: the discovery of subatomic particles (electrons by Thomson, protons and the nucleus by Rutherford, neutrons by Chadwick) was crucial in helping scientists understand the structure of the atom. Each discovery led to a better and more accurate model.

The Reason is also true: in a neutral atom the number of electrons is always equal to the number of protons. This is a fundamental fact that keeps atoms electrically neutral.

However, R is not the correct explanation of A. The equal number of electrons and protons is a consequence of atomic neutrality — it tells us about the balance of charge in an atom. It does not explain how or why the discovery of subatomic particles helped in understanding atomic structure. That discovery helped because it revealed what atoms are made of, how their mass is distributed and how the particles are arranged — not merely because electrons equal protons.

Revise, Reflect, Refine · Q7
8 Magnesium is essential for many biological processes, including muscle contraction. For an atom of magnesium with a mass number of 24 and atomic number 12, determine the number of (i) protons, (ii) neutrons, (iii) electrons, and also illustrate the arrangement of electrons in a magnesium atom.

Mass number (A) = 24, Atomic number (Z) = 12.

  • (i) Protons = atomic number = 12
  • (ii) Neutrons = mass number − atomic number = 24 − 12 = 12
  • (iii) Electrons = number of protons (the atom is neutral) = 12

Arrangement of electrons (electronic configuration):

  • K-shell (n = 1): 2 electrons — maximum 2, filled first
  • L-shell (n = 2): 8 electrons — maximum 8, filled next
  • M-shell (n = 3): 2 electrons — the remaining electrons
Electronic configuration of magnesium: 2, 8, 2

Revise, Reflect, Refine · Q9
10 Both Rutherford's and Bohr's models have electrons orbiting the nucleus. Why did Rutherford's model fail to explain atomic stability, while Bohr's model succeeded?

The key difference lies in how each model describes the motion of the electron.

Rutherford's model and why it failed: in his model electrons orbit the nucleus freely, like planets around the Sun. But according to the laws of physics (as you saw in the chapter on describing circular motion), a particle moving in a circular path is constantly changing direction, which means it is accelerating. A negatively charged particle that is accelerating should continuously emit energy in the form of radiation. If electrons kept losing energy they would slow down, spiral inward towards the nucleus and finally fall into it. Atoms would then collapse in a very short time and could not exist in a stable form. Rutherford's model had no explanation for why this does not happen.

Bohr's model and why it succeeded: Bohr introduced the concept of stationary states (fixed orbits/shells). He proposed that when an electron is in a fixed allowed orbit, its energy remains constant — it does not emit or lose energy while in that orbit. This was a postulate (an assumed rule) introduced specifically to remove Rutherford's limitation. By placing electrons in fixed energy levels where energy is constant, Bohr's model could explain why electrons do not spiral inward and why atoms are stable. It could also explain many experimental observations, such as the spectrum of hydrogen.

Revise, Reflect, Refine · Q10
11 An atom ⁷⁰X has 31 electrons. How many neutrons are there in its nucleus?

Given: mass number (A) = 70 (the superscript) and number of electrons = 31.

Since the atom is neutral, the number of protons = number of electrons = 31, so the atomic number Z = 31.

Number of neutrons = A − Z = 70 − 31 = 39.

Answer: There are 39 neutrons in its nucleus. (This element is gallium, Ga.)
Revise, Reflect, Refine · Q11
12 An atom has 79 protons and a mass number of 197. Calculate (i) the number of neutrons and (ii) the number of electrons.

Given: number of protons (atomic number, Z) = 79; mass number (A) = 197.

(i) Number of neutrons = mass number − number of protons = 197 − 79 = 118.

(ii) Since the atom is electrically neutral, number of electrons = number of protons = 79.

Answer: (i) 118 neutrons   (ii) 79 electrons. (This element is gold, Au.)

Revise, Reflect, Refine · Q13
14 Aman was discussing the structure of the atom with his classmates. During the discussion he learnt that an element X has a mass number of 35 and contains 18 neutrons. Based on this information, answer the following questions:
(i) How many electrons and protons does element X have?
(ii) What is its atomic number?
(iii) Identify the element X.
(iv) Write its electronic configuration.
(v) How many valence electrons does it have?
(vi) What will be the mass number if two neutrons are added to its nucleus?
(vii) What will be the relation of X with the new atom?

(i) Mass number = 35, neutrons = 18. Protons = mass number − neutrons = 35 − 18 = 17. Since the atom is neutral, electrons = protons = 17.

(ii) Atomic number = number of protons = 17.

(iii) The element with atomic number 17 is chlorine (Cl).

(iv) 17 electrons fill as K = 2, L = 8, M = 7. Electronic configuration = 2, 8, 7.

(v) The outermost (valence) shell is the M-shell, which has 7 valence electrons.

(vi) Original neutrons = 18; adding 2 more gives 20 neutrons. New mass number = protons + new neutrons = 17 + 20 = 37.

(vii) The original element X has mass number 35 and atomic number 17. The new atom has mass number 37 but the same atomic number 17 (the protons have not changed). Both are chlorine with the same atomic number but different mass numbers, so X and the new atom are isotopes of each other.

Revise, Reflect, Refine · Q14
15 In an atom there are 12 protons and 12 neutrons in the nucleus. Now imagine that all the electrons are replaced with some hypothetical particles that have the same charge as electrons but are 500 times heavier. What effect will this replacement have on the atom's: (i) atomic number, (ii) atomic mass, (iii) mass number, (iv) overall charge?

(i) Atomic number: the atomic number is decided by the number of protons in the nucleus. The protons are unchanged (we only replaced electrons), so the atomic number stays the same = 12.

(ii) Atomic mass: it increases. Normally electrons have negligible mass, but particles 500 times heavier add a significant amount of extra mass to the atom.

(iii) Mass number: mass number = protons + neutrons = 12 + 12 = 24. Both protons and neutrons are unchanged, and electrons are not counted in the mass number, so it stays the same.

(iv) Overall charge: no change. The hypothetical particles have the same charge as electrons (charge −1 each), and the number of them still equals the original number of electrons (12). So the total negative charge is still −12, while the protons give +12. The atom remains electrically neutral.

🔢Section B — Numericals (P1–P9)

P1Numerical
Question: An atom with an atomic number of 26 has 56 nucleons. Find out its number of electrons, protons and neutrons.

Given:

  • Atomic number (Z) = 26
  • Total nucleons (mass number A) = 56

To find:

  • Number of protons
  • Number of electrons
  • Number of neutrons
Solution:
  1. Protons = atomic number = 26
  2. The atom is electrically neutral, so electrons = protons = 26
  3. Neutrons = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30

Answer: 26 protons, 26 electrons, 30 neutrons. (This element is iron, Fe.)

P2Numerical
Question: The nucleus of an atom contains 20 protons. If its mass number is 41, find the number of neutrons in it.

Given:

  • Number of protons = 20
  • Mass number (A) = 41

To find:

  • Number of neutrons
Solution:
  1. Neutrons = mass number − number of protons
  2. = 41 − 20 = 21

Answer: The atom has 21 neutrons.

P3Numerical
Question: An atom has 18 neutrons and an atomic number of 17. What is its mass number?

Given:

  • Number of neutrons = 18
  • Atomic number (protons) = 17

To find:

  • Mass number (A)
Solution:
  1. A = number of protons + number of neutrons
  2. = 17 + 18 = 35

Answer: The mass number is 35. (This element is chlorine, Cl.)

P4Numerical
Question: An atom ²³A has 11 electrons. Find the number of neutrons in it.

Given:

  • Mass number (A) = 23
  • Number of electrons = 11

To find:

  • Number of neutrons
Solution:
  1. The atom is neutral, so protons = electrons = 11
  2. Neutrons = mass number − number of protons = 23 − 11 = 12

Answer: The atom has 12 neutrons. (This element is sodium, Na.)

Question: Solve this riddle: I am an atom with a mass number of 23 and 11 protons. I am a soft metal and I react vigorously with water. Who am I and how many neutrons do I have? (You can also create one such riddle.)

Given:

  • Mass number = 23
  • Number of protons (atomic number) = 11

To find:

  • Element and neutrons
Solution:
  1. Neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12
  2. The element with atomic number 11 is sodium (Na)
  3. Sodium is indeed a soft metal that reacts vigorously with water

Answer: I am sodium (Na) and I have 12 neutrons.

P6Numerical
Question: Two different atoms have 11 protons each, but one has 12 neutrons and the other has 13 neutrons. How do their atomic numbers and mass numbers compare? Are they the same element or different elements?

Given:

  • Both atoms: 11 protons each
  • Neutrons: 12 and 13

To find:

  • Atomic numbers
  • Mass numbers
  • Whether they are the same element
Solution:
  1. Both have 11 protons, so both have atomic number = 11
  2. Mass number of the first = 11 + 12 = 23; of the second = 11 + 13 = 24
  3. Same atomic number → same element, sodium (Na)
  4. Different mass numbers → they are isotopes of sodium

Answer: Same atomic number (11) but different mass numbers (23 and 24). They are the same element — isotopes of sodium.

P7Numerical
Question: If a bromine atom is available in the form of two isotopes, ⁷⁹₃₅Br (49.7%) and ⁸¹₃₅Br (50.3%), calculate the average atomic mass of the bromine atom.

Given:

  • Isotope 1: mass 79 u, abundance 49.7%
  • Isotope 2: mass 81 u, abundance 50.3%

To find:

  • Average (weighted) atomic mass
Solution:
  1. Weighted average = (mass of first isotope × its %/100) + (mass of second isotope × its %/100)
  2. = (79 × 49.7/100) + (81 × 50.3/100)
  3. = (79 × 0.497) + (81 × 0.503) = 39.263 + 40.743 = 80.006 u

Answer: The average atomic mass of bromine is approximately 80 u.

P8Numerical
Question: Write the electronic configuration of the elements having atomic numbers 12, 16 and 18.

Given:

  • Z = 12, 16 and 18

To find:

  • Electronic configuration of each element
Solution:
  1. Z = 12 (Magnesium): K = 2, L = 8, M = 2 → 2, 8, 2
  2. Z = 16 (Sulfur): K = 2, L = 8, M = 6 → 2, 8, 6
  3. Z = 18 (Argon): K = 2, L = 8, M = 8 → 2, 8, 8

Answer: Mg: 2, 8, 2  ·  S: 2, 8, 6  ·  Ar: 2, 8, 8

P9Numerical
Question: Identify the number of electrons in the outermost shell of the following elements: (i) ¹²₆C   (ii) ¹⁹₉F   (iii) ²⁸₁₄Si

Given:

  • Carbon: Z = 6
  • Fluorine: Z = 9
  • Silicon: Z = 14

To find:

  • Number of valence electrons of each
Solution:
  1. Carbon: configuration 2, 4 → outermost shell is L with 4 electrons
  2. Fluorine: configuration 2, 7 → outermost shell is L with 7 electrons
  3. Silicon: configuration 2, 8, 4 → outermost shell is M with 4 electrons

Answer: C → 4, F → 7, Si → 4 valence electrons
