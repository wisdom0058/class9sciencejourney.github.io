📘Lesson — How Elements Got Their Short Names

First, pictures (1803)

John Dalton was the first to introduce symbols for elements, in 1803. He used small pictorial signs — circles with dots and lines inside them. These were hard to draw and hard to print, so scientists needed something simpler.

Then, letters (1813)

In 1813 Berzelius suggested that symbols should be made from the letters of the element's name, often its Latin name. This gave us the alphabetic signs we use today. Nowadays all names and symbols are approved by IUPAC — the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry.

Rules for writing a symbol (IUPAC norms)

1️⃣ First letter is CAPITAL

Hydrogen → H, Carbon → C, Nitrogen → N.

2️⃣ Second letter is small

If the symbol has two letters, the second must be lowercase: Cobalt → Co (not CO), Aluminium → Al (not AL).

3️⃣ First + another letter is allowed

Sometimes a letter other than the second is used: Chlorine → Cl, Zinc → Zn (that is why it is not Z).

4️⃣ Latin, Greek or German roots

Some symbols come from old names: Iron → Fe (Ferrum), Gold → Au (Aurum).

Why standard symbols? A symbol is understood by scientists all over the world, whatever language they speak. “Fe” means iron in every country. Without standard symbols, chemical equations and formulas would become a mess of different private notations and research could not be shared.

💬Ask & Understand

Q1Who introduced symbols of elements for the first time, and who made them alphabetic?Very short answer▾

John Dalton was the first to introduce symbols for elements, in 1803, using pictorial representations. In 1813 Berzelius suggested that symbols should be derived from the Latin names of elements, which gave rise to the alphabetic chemical symbols we use today.

Q2Pause & Ponder: Imagine you have discovered a new element. Name it after yourself and justify that your symbol follows IUPAC rules.Pause & Ponder▾

Example: let the scientist's name be “Aryan”. The element could be called Aryanium with the symbol Ay.

Justification: the first letter ‘A’ is written as a capital — follows IUPAC rules; the second letter ‘y’ is written in lowercase — follows IUPAC rules; the symbol uses the first two letters of the element's name; and the combination “Ay” does not clash with an existing element symbol.

Q3What problems could arise if every scientist used a different symbol for the same element?Pause & Ponder▾

There would be great confusion in scientific communication worldwide.

  • Scientists from different countries could not understand each other's papers and findings.
  • Chemical equations and formulas would become inconsistent and unreadable.
  • Errors could occur in experiments, industry and medicine — which can be dangerous.
  • International collaboration and sharing of knowledge would become impossible.
  • The same element could have hundreds of different symbols, so there would be no universal language of science.
Q4Give five examples of elements whose symbols come from languages other than English.Short answer▾

• Iron → Fe (Latin Ferrum)  • Copper → Cu (Latin Cuprum)  • Gold → Au (Latin Aurum)  • Mercury → Hg (Greek Hydrargyros)  • Tungsten → W (German Wolfram).

📘Book Questions — Explained

1Explain the rules for writing symbols of elements as per IUPAC norms. Why are standard symbols necessary? Give five examples of elements whose symbols are derived from languages other than English.

Rules: many symbols are the first letter or the first two letters of the element's name (Hydrogen → H, Aluminium → Al). The first letter is always capital, the second letter (if any) is always lowercase (Cobalt → Co, not CO). Some symbols use the first letter and another prominent letter of the name (Chlorine → Cl, Zinc → Zn). Some symbols come from Latin, Greek or German names instead of English.

Why needed: standard symbols are internationally recognised, so scientists everywhere can communicate clearly regardless of language barriers.

Five examples: Fe (iron, Latin Ferrum), Cu (copper, Latin Cuprum), Au (gold, Latin Aurum), Hg (mercury, Greek Hydrargyros), W (tungsten, German Wolfram).

Long answer
2Why do we need symbols for elements at all? What is the difference between the symbol and the name?

The name of an element is long and differs from language to language, but a symbol is a short, universal, one- or two-letter code. Symbols make it easy to write chemical formulas (H₂O, NaCl) and equations quickly and correctly, and they are understood in every country. A symbol never changes, while names can be translated — for example, iron is “loha” in Hindi but its symbol is always Fe.

Question bank
3Write the symbols of hydrogen, helium, lithium, boron, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, neon, sodium, magnesium, aluminium, silicon, phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine, argon, potassium and calcium.

H, He, Li, B, C, N, O, F, Ne, Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar, K, Ca. Notice carefully: sodium is Na (not So), potassium is K (not P, because P is phosphorus), and silicon is Si (not S, because S is sulfur).
