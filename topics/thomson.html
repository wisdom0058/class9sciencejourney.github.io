Discovery of the Electron & Thomson's Model

Cathode rays, the electron, and the famous plum-pudding model of the atom.

Cathode raysElectron −1Plum pudding
📘 Lesson🧪 Demo Lab🎯 Quick Quiz💬 Ask & Understand📘 Book Questions

📘Lesson — Cathode Rays, the Electron and the Plum Pudding

Step 1: Passing electricity through a gas

In 1897 J. J. Thomson studied how electric current passes through gases at very low pressure. He took a glass tube fitted with two electrodes and applied a very high voltage. When the pressure inside was reduced a lot, the tube glowed with a faint greenish light.

He also made a hole in the anode and coated the tube behind it with a fluorescent material like ZnS. A bright spot appeared on the coating. Something invisible was travelling from the cathode (negative electrode) towards the anode (positive electrode). These rays were called cathode rays.

💡
Thomson first called these particles corpuscles of negative electricity. Later they were named electrons, on the suggestion of G. J. Stoney, meaning “particles or atoms of electricity”.

Step 2: What did Thomson find out?

  • By studying the rays in electric and magnetic fields, he showed that cathode rays are streams of negatively charged particles.
  • These particles have a mass much smaller than an atom.
  • The rays were the same whatever material the cathode was made of and whatever gas was filled in the tube.

⚡ Charge of an electron

−1.602 × 10−19 C. In chemistry we simply take it as −1 unit, as a matter of convention.

⚖️ Mass of an electron

9.11 × 10−31 kg — about 1/1840 times the mass of a hydrogen atom. So an electron is almost massless compared with an atom.

🌍
Why this mattered: because every element he tried gave the same electrons, Thomson concluded that electrons are present in the atoms of all elements — they are a fundamental component of matter.

Step 3: But wait — atoms are neutral!

If atoms contain negative electrons, and an atom as a whole is electrically neutral, then there must also be some positive charge somewhere inside the atom to balance the electrons.

Thomson suggested: an atom is a sphere of uniform positive charge, and the negatively charged electrons are embedded in it, like plums in a pudding or seeds in a watermelon.

🍮 Plum pudding model

The positive sphere is the pudding; the electrons are the plums (raisins) stuck inside it.

🍉 Watermelon comparison

The red pulp stands for the positive charge, and the seeds stand for the electrons.

This was the first genuine attempt to explain how an atom's positive and negative charges stay balanced. It explained electrical neutrality, but it could not explain the results of Rutherford's gold foil experiment — as you will see in the next topic.

Set the voltage and switch the magnet on and off. Watch the beam start at the cathode, travel to the anode, and bend when a magnetic field is applied. Change the cathode material and the gas — notice that the rays stay exactly the same, which is why Thomson said electrons are present in all atoms.

💬Ask & Understand

Q1What are cathode rays?Very short answer▾

Cathode rays are streams of negatively charged particles (electrons) that move from the cathode (negative electrode) to the anode (positive electrode) in a discharge tube.

Q2Why is Thomson's model called the plum pudding model?Very short answer▾

Thomson proposed the atom as a sphere of positive charge with electrons distributed throughout it — just like plums (raisins) embedded in a pudding. So the model is called the plum pudding model. The same idea is compared with a watermelon: red pulp = positive charge, seeds = electrons.

Q3Why did Thomson conclude that electrons are present in all atoms?Short answer▾

Cathode rays (streams of electrons) were produced regardless of the material used for the cathode or the gas filled in the tube. Since the same negatively charged particles came out of any material under any condition, Thomson concluded that electrons are a fundamental and common component present in all atoms of all elements.

Q4Pause & Ponder: You make your own Thomson atom with clay for the positive charge and beads for electrons. (i) What if the positive charge is less than the total negative charge? (ii) What if the clay itself carries a bit of negative charge — is it still a neutral atom?Numerical / reasoning▾

(i) The atom would not be electrically neutral. It would have a net negative charge and behave like a negative ion. In a real atom the total positive charge always equals the total negative charge.

(ii) No. Now both the clay and the beads are negative, so there is no positive charge left to balance them. The model becomes entirely negative and does not represent a neutral atom.

Q5Could an orange or a lemon be a good comparison for Thomson's model? Where does it fall short?Pause & Ponder▾

Where it matches: the seeds embedded in the soft pulp look like electrons inside the positive sphere, and the fruit is roughly spherical like an atom.

Where it falls short: in Thomson's model the electrons are spread uniformly through the positive sphere, while the seeds of an orange are clustered in compartments. Also the pulp is not uniformly positively charged — it is just neutral fruit pulp. So it is a nice picture, but not an accurate model.

📘Book Questions — Explained

1How did J. J. Thomson discover the electron? What did he conclude about the nature of cathode rays?

In 1897 Thomson passed electricity through gases at very low pressure in a glass tube with two electrodes. He saw rays travelling from the cathode to the anode — cathode rays.

Studying them in electric and magnetic fields, he concluded that cathode rays are streams of negatively charged particles much lighter than atoms. He named them electrons. Since the rays were independent of the cathode material and the gas, electrons are a fundamental part of all atoms. The charge of an electron is taken as −1 (i.e. −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C).

Short answer
2Describe Thomson's model of the atom. Why is it also called the plum pudding model?

After discovering electrons, Thomson faced a puzzle: electrons are negative but atoms are neutral, so positive charge must exist somewhere. He proposed that an atom is a sphere of uniform positive charge with electrons distributed throughout it.

The positive sphere is like a pudding and the electrons like plums inside it, hence the name plum pudding model. A simpler comparison is a watermelon: red pulp = positive charge, seeds = electrons. It was the first model to explain how positive and negative charges stay balanced, but Rutherford's gold foil experiment later disproved it.

Short answer
3Case-based: (a) What are the negatively charged particles discovered by Thomson called, and what is the charge of one such particle? (b) Describe Thomson's model and explain why it is called the plum pudding model. (c) In the cathode ray tube, towards which electrode do cathode rays travel, and why?

(a) They are called electrons. The charge of one electron is −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, taken as −1 as a matter of convention.

(b) Thomson proposed the atom to be a sphere of positive charge with electrons distributed throughout it. It is called the plum pudding model because the positive sphere was compared to a pudding and the electrons to plums (raisins) embedded in it.

(c) Cathode rays travel from the cathode (negative electrode) towards the anode (positive electrode), because they are negatively charged particles and are attracted towards the positive electrode.
